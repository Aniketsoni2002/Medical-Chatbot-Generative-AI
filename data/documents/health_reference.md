## Common Cold and Influenza (Flu)

The common cold and influenza are viral respiratory infections.

- **Cold symptoms:** runny or stuffy nose, sneezing, mild sore throat, mild cough,
  usually no high fever. Onset is gradual.
- **Flu symptoms:** sudden fever, chills, body aches, fatigue, headache, dry cough,
  and sore throat. Symptoms are typically more severe than a cold.
- **Self-care:** rest, fluids, and over-the-counter symptom relief as directed on
  the label. Most people recover in 7–10 days.
- **Seek care if:** difficulty breathing, chest pain, persistent high fever,
  dehydration, or symptoms that worsen after initial improvement.

## Fever

Fever is a temporary rise in body temperature, often due to infection. A normal
temperature is around 37°C (98.6°F).

- **Home care:** rest, fluids, light clothing. Antipyretic medicines can help
  comfort — follow label dosing.
- **Seek urgent care if:** fever above 39.4°C (103°F), fever lasting more than
  three days, a stiff neck, confusion, rash, difficulty breathing, or fever in an
  infant under three months.

## High Blood Pressure (Hypertension)

Blood pressure is recorded as systolic/diastolic (mmHg).

- **Normal:** below 120/80.
- **Elevated:** systolic 120–129 and diastolic below 80.
- **Stage 1 hypertension:** 130–139 systolic or 80–89 diastolic.
- **Stage 2 hypertension:** 140/90 or higher.
- **Hypertensive crisis:** higher than 180/120 — seek emergency care, especially
  with chest pain, shortness of breath, or vision changes.

**Management** commonly includes reducing salt, regular physical activity,
maintaining a healthy weight, limiting alcohol, not smoking, managing stress, and
taking prescribed medication as directed. Do not stop or change blood pressure
medication without consulting a healthcare provider.

## Diabetes

Diabetes affects how the body regulates blood sugar (glucose).

- **HbA1c** reflects average blood sugar over about three months. Many adults with
  diabetes aim for below 7%, but individual targets vary — a provider sets them.
- **Type 2 management** often includes healthy eating, physical activity, weight
  management, monitoring glucose, and medication when prescribed.
- **Warning signs of low blood sugar (hypoglycemia):** shakiness, sweating,
  confusion, rapid heartbeat, and hunger. Treat with fast-acting carbohydrate.
- **Warning signs of high blood sugar:** excessive thirst, frequent urination,
  fatigue, and blurred vision.

## Asthma and COPD

Both are chronic conditions affecting the airways.

- **Asthma** causes reversible airway narrowing with wheezing, coughing, chest
  tightness, and shortness of breath, often triggered by allergens or exercise.
- **COPD** (chronic obstructive pulmonary disease) is progressive and often linked
  to smoking. Common symptoms are chronic cough, mucus, and breathlessness.
- **General guidance:** follow your action plan, avoid known triggers, use inhalers
  as prescribed, and don't smoke.
- **Seek emergency care** for severe breathlessness, blue lips or face, or a rescue
  inhaler that isn't helping.

## Body Mass Index (BMI)

BMI estimates body fat from weight and height (kg/m²).

- **Underweight:** below 18.5
- **Healthy weight:** 18.5–24.9
- **Overweight:** 25.0–29.9
- **Obesity:** 30.0 and above

BMI is a screening tool, not a diagnosis; it does not account for muscle mass or
body composition.

## First Aid Basics

### Minor Burns
Cool the burn under cool (not ice-cold) running water for about 20 minutes. Remove
tight items nearby. Cover loosely with a clean, non-stick dressing. Do not apply
butter or ice. Seek care for large, deep, or facial burns, or burns on children.

### Minor Cuts and Scrapes
Wash your hands, apply gentle pressure to stop bleeding, clean the wound with water,
apply a clean dressing. Seek care for deep wounds, wounds that won't stop bleeding,
or signs of infection (increasing redness, warmth, pus, fever).

### Nosebleeds
Sit upright and lean slightly forward. Pinch the soft part of the nose for 10–15
minutes and breathe through your mouth. Seek care if bleeding won't stop after
20 minutes or follows a serious injury.

### Choking
If a person can cough or speak, encourage coughing. If they cannot breathe, call
emergency services and give back blows and abdominal thrusts if trained.

### CPR Overview
If someone is unresponsive and not breathing normally, call emergency services and
begin chest compressions (push hard and fast in the center of the chest). Use an
AED if available. Formal CPR training is strongly recommended.

## Recognizing Medical Emergencies

Call emergency services immediately for:

- Chest pain or pressure, especially spreading to the arm, jaw, or back
- Sudden difficulty breathing
- Signs of stroke: face drooping, arm weakness, slurred speech (act F.A.S.T.)
- Severe or uncontrolled bleeding
- Loss of consciousness, seizures, or severe allergic reaction (anaphylaxis)

## Preventive Care and Healthy Living

- **Diet:** emphasize vegetables, fruit, whole grains, lean protein, and healthy
  fats. Limit added sugar, salt, and ultra-processed foods.
- **Physical activity:** aim for about 150 minutes of moderate activity per week
  plus muscle-strengthening twice weekly, as able.
- **Sleep:** most adults need 7–9 hours nightly.
- **Hydration:** drink water regularly; needs vary with activity and climate.
- **Vaccinations and screenings:** follow the schedule recommended by your provider.
- **Avoid tobacco** and limit alcohol.

## Mental Health and Stress

- Regular exercise, sleep, social connection, and mindfulness can support mental
  well-being.
- Simple stress techniques include slow breathing, short walks, and breaking tasks
  into smaller steps.
- Persistent low mood, anxiety, or loss of interest deserves professional support.
- If you have thoughts of harming yourself, seek immediate help from emergency
  services or a crisis line.

## Medication Safety (General)

- Take medicines exactly as prescribed; do not change doses on your own.
- Read labels for side effects, interactions, and storage instructions.
- Keep an up-to-date list of your medicines and share it with your providers.
- Ask a pharmacist or provider about missed doses rather than doubling up.

## When to See a Doctor

See a healthcare professional for symptoms that are severe, persistent, worsening,
or unexplained; for new or changing lumps; for chronic-condition management; and for
routine preventive care and screenings.
